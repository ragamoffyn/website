2022 · Data Visualization · Decision Support

National Security

Highlighting patterns in heterogeneous risk data for analysts performing work critical to national security.

The Project

Risk analysts at a national security organization needed a way to find patterns across large volumes of unconnected data — information that existed in multiple heterogeneous sources but had no common interface through which it could be explored together. The goal was to reduce both the time and the effort required to perform that analysis while simultaneously extracting more value from the data the organization already had. The strategy was a common, dynamic threat picture: a visualization that would draw from multiple information sources and surface connections between them that would otherwise go unnoticed.

Background and Context

The client performs analyses that are critical to national security. A recent and significant increase in workload had created two related pressures: a need to increase throughput, and a need to get more value from the data already available. The project team included developers, risk engineers, and me as the UX practitioner.

There were a number of existing back-end tools that could, in combination, support a meaningful solution — but no user interface tied them together, and the analysts had no existing visualization or interface of any kind to work from. The work I was brought in to do was to provide something new, starting from a blank slate.

My Role

I served as the UX lead on a cross-company team. That responsibility covered the full range of UX activities on the project: all user research, interaction design, and prototyping. I was the sole practitioner on the UX side, working alongside a team of developers and risk engineers on both the client and vendor sides.

Constraints

The client's requirements for the deliverable were specific and, in combination, demanding. The interactive prototype needed to demonstrate, credibly, that the proposed solution could reduce analysis time and improve risk detection. It also needed to look polished enough to convince the funding agency that the concept was feasible — not a rough sketch, but something that read as a real product. And it needed to be demonstrable within a few minutes in a scripted context.

The deadline left me with approximately 100 hours over the course of 5 weeks for all research, design, and prototyping work. That is a very limited window for the scope of what was being asked.

The Approach

The first and perhaps most important step was managing client expectations. The ambitions the client had brought to the project exceeded what the timeline would support. Rather than attempting to meet those ambitions and falling short, I reframed the project as an initial step in a larger effort — a proof of concept that would establish the direction and demonstrate feasibility, not a finished product.

The second step was tight scoping. Working with both the client and my own team, I defined a very specific scenario: a single incident, a sequence of activities the analyst would perform in response to it, and a clear endpoint. This level of specificity was essential given the time constraints. It meant I could focus my research on only those user actions, decisions, and needs that fell within that scenario. It meant I could design only the portions of the interface that scenario would actually encounter. And it meant I could add only as much interactivity as was needed to support a scripted demonstration — no more.

For the prototype itself, I used Figma and worked from a design template that had sufficient interactive capabilities for the scripted walkthrough, which allowed me to save time that would otherwise have gone to building foundational components from scratch.

Results

Despite the limited hours available for user interviews and a very compressed design window, the prototype met the client's stated needs and was well received. The actual design work is confidential and cannot be shared publicly, but the outcome of the process was a successful demonstration that satisfied both the client and the funding agency. The project delivered what it needed to deliver within both the time and budget constraints.

Challenges and Lessons

This project involved the largest set of compromises I had faced to that point in my career. Both the research and the design were far from ideal given the time available — user research was minimal, and design iteration was necessarily limited. However, the combination of early expectation management, tight scenario scoping, and a clear focus on a single use case produced something that satisfied the client.

The central lesson was one of scope: when time and money are limited, the most important thing is to define a very small, very specific target — and then hit it cleanly. Attempting to cover more ground with fewer resources produces something that pleases no one.
