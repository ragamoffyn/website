2012 — 2013 · Mobile UX · Game Design

Mobile Games

UX consulting for a San Francisco startup developing turn-based strategy tablet games.

Case study visuals available on request

The Context

Threadbare was an Agile startup in San Francisco developing a line of tablet games with a focus on the turn-based strategy genre. The studio was operating with limited funding, and the goal was to produce minimum viable versions of games as quickly as possible — identifying a commercially viable product and beginning to generate revenue before resources ran out. I was brought on as a UX consultant to help ensure that the games being built were not only usable and learnable, but genuinely fun and something people would want to spend money on.

My Role

My primary responsibility was to represent the player experience across the development process. That meant attending both to the traditional UX concerns — usability, learnability, clarity of feedback and affordances — and to the game-specific concern of engagement: whether the game was enjoyable to play in a way that would bring players back and motivate them to spend money. Ensuring that UX considerations received adequate attention in an environment where development priorities frequently had to take precedence was a consistent part of the role.

The Process

The work followed an Agile sprint model, condensed compared to non-Agile environments. The first couple of weeks were used by the development team to come up to speed with iOS, while I studied the research literature on what makes games enjoyable — identifying the game elements and design properties most strongly associated with engagement and returning player behavior. I produced a set of game design principles and elements that could be built into the designs and referenced throughout the development cycle.

After that initial period, the work proceeded as a series of sprints. Each sprint typically began with the development team working on core code while I laid out the basic UI for the next phase of the game, working at a pace that could keep ahead of implementation without overspecifying features that might change.

Activities

The range of UX activities across the project included:

  • Page flows and navigation maps
  • Storyboards and usage scenarios
  • Wireframes and mockups
  • Interactive prototypes (Axure)
  • Game tutorial design
  • Remote usability testing
  • User stories
  • Design principles documentation

Google Docs served as the primary creation and collaboration tool across most of these activities — chosen because it provided adequate collaborative capabilities at no additional cost, which mattered in a resource-constrained environment. Axure was used for interactive prototypes.

Results

One game was released during the engagement and received favorable reviews from players, though it did not achieve commercial success at a scale that could sustain the studio. A second game reached mid-stage development before being put on hold due to technical challenges. A third reached early-stage development before the company shut down. The outcomes were mixed, as is common in early-stage game development, but the process produced work that was well-received by the people who played it.

Challenges and Lessons

The most persistent challenge was the pressure that very limited resources placed on UX priorities. Development concerns — what could be built, in what time, with what technical constraints — consistently had to take precedence, and the UX work had to find ways to be useful within those boundaries rather than insisting on ideal conditions that were not available. This was a real limitation, though one that is characteristic of early-stage startup work rather than something specific to this project.

Working remotely from Norway while the team was based in San Francisco added a layer of difficulty as well. The time zone difference meant that synchronous collaboration required careful scheduling, and the distance made it harder to maintain the informal communication that tends to keep design and development well-aligned in co-located teams. That said, good communication and a mutual understanding of each other's constraints went a considerable way toward making it work.

The central lessons from this engagement were two. First, co-location is genuinely preferable for Agile work — the feedback loops are faster and the informal coordination that keeps a team aligned is much easier to sustain in person. Second, when co-location is not possible, clear communication and a shared understanding of constraints are not optional — they are what make distributed Agile work at all.
