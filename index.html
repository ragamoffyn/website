About me

My educational background is in cognitive psychology, which is essentially the study of human mental capabilities and limitations. I had a particular focus on human learning and decision making. It was not originally intended as preparation for UX work, but it has proven well-suited to it — and it continues to inform how I approach both the research and the design sides of my work.

Over the course of my career, I have worked in a number of different domains and on a number of quite different products, including military decision support systems, air traffic control, crisis management platforms, precision agriculture tools, and offshore energy software, among others. My expertise and passion, however, is in the research and design of decision-support tools for professionals — particularly those working in complex, dynamic environments where their decisions carry real consequences.

My work typically involves both user research and interface design, which I find to be an advantage over specializing in one or the other. Research shapes what gets designed, and the act of designing tends to raise new research questions. The two inform each other in ways that are difficult to replicate when they are treated as separate activities.

Currently I am based in Oslo, where I lead UX for a digital solutions platform in the offshore energy sector. The work involves establishing and refining the company's design system, improving the overall coherence of the platform, and expanding user research across a number of product teams.

Context

4Subsea develops monitoring and integrity management software for the offshore energy industry — tools used by engineers who are responsible for assets in physically demanding and high-stakes environments. I have been the sole UX practitioner on the team, which means my responsibilities cover the full scope of UX: from individual component design to the overall structure and consistency of the platform.

Challenge

The platform had grown product by product, without a unified design foundation to maintain consistency across it. The result was a degree of variation in interaction patterns, terminology, and navigation that created unnecessary friction for users who move between products regularly. At the same time, the company was expanding its user base, which made addressing those inconsistencies more urgent.

Constraints

Working as a solo practitioner in an engineering-led organization involves managing a number of competing priorities. The users are experienced engineers with well-developed opinions about their tools and limited availability for formal research sessions, which meant finding ways to embed research into existing workflows rather than staging it separately.

What I Did

  • Adopted and refined the company's first design system — shared components, patterns, documentation — bringing consistency across products.
  • Led a platform-level usability redesign: auditing products, improving navigation models, information hierarchy, and data display conventions.
  • Embedded user research across product teams, making research a shared practice rather than a siloed function.

What I Learned

Getting a design system adopted requires as much effort on the communication side as on the design side — perhaps more. The users in this context also reinforced something I have observed in a number of other expert-user settings: they are more tolerant of interface complexity than of inconsistency. They will navigate a dense interface if the underlying logic is clear; what they find frustrating is when similar things behave differently for no apparent reason.

AI & UX

AI in the Research & Design Process

AI is changing a significant portion of the UX process, and keeping pace with that requires active effort. I have been paying particular attention to the areas below — both as a practitioner exploring how AI tools can support my own work, and as a designer thinking about what it means to design for products that incorporate AI.

AI-Assisted Research

There is a good deal of value in using AI to support qualitative research synthesis — identifying themes across interview transcripts, comparing findings across sessions, and flagging potential gaps in coverage. The part that still requires a trained researcher is evaluating what those patterns mean and whether the interpretations they suggest hold up against what was actually observed.

AI-Augmented Design

Generative tools can accelerate the ideation phase of design work considerably. Where they are less reliable is in decisions that depend on a real understanding of the user's context. AI can produce variations quickly, but determining which of those variations are appropriate still requires someone with familiarity with the work the users are actually doing.

Designing AI Experiences

Designing for AI-powered features involves some specific UX challenges, largely because the system's behavior is not fully predictable. This affects how one thinks about trust, feedback, and error handling. Users need enough transparency to calibrate their confidence in the system appropriately, without that transparency itself becoming a source of cognitive overhead.
