About

A cognitive psychologist who became a designer

I have a Ph.D. in cognitive psychology, which meant spending years studying how people form mental models, process information, and make decisions under uncertainty. That training wasn't originally aimed at UX — but it turned out to be good preparation for it.

Since then I've worked across a range of domains that don't obviously belong together: military decision support, air traffic control, crisis management, banking, precision agriculture, and offshore energy operations. The connecting thread has been the same kind of user in each case — professionals doing skilled, consequential work who need tools that respect their expertise rather than simplify it away.

I tend to do both research and design, which in my experience is an advantage. Research without design is just analysis; design without research is guesswork. The work I find most interesting is where those two things inform each other in real time.

At the moment I'm in Oslo, leading UX for a software platform in the offshore energy sector. The work is mostly about bringing coherence to a product suite that's grown quickly — building a design system, improving platform consistency, and embedding research into teams that haven't always had a UX presence.

Context

4Subsea makes software for the offshore energy industry — monitoring and integrity management tools used by engineers tracking assets in demanding physical environments. I've been the sole UX practitioner on the team, which means being involved in decisions that range from individual component behavior to the overall structure of the platform.

Challenge

The platform had grown product by product, without a shared design foundation. Terms meant different things in different tools, navigation behaved inconsistently, and the visual language varied. For users who move between products regularly — and these users do — that created friction that wasn't serving anyone. At the same time the company was bringing on more users, which raised the stakes for getting the experience right.

Constraints

Working alone in an engineering-driven organization means navigating a lot of competing priorities. The users are experienced engineers with strong opinions about their tools and limited time for formal research sessions — which pushed me toward embedding research into existing meetings and reviews rather than staging it separately.

What I Did

  • Adopted and refined the company's first design system — shared components, patterns, documentation — bringing consistency across products.
  • Led a platform-level usability redesign: auditing products, improving navigation models, information hierarchy, and data display conventions.
  • Embedded user research across product teams, making research a shared practice rather than a siloed function.

What I Learned

A design system is only as useful as people's willingness to use it, which turns out to depend as much on how you communicate it as how you build it. The users also reinforced something I've noticed across other expert-user contexts: they're not bothered by density or complexity as long as the logic is consistent. What bothers them is when similar things behave differently for no apparent reason.

AI & UX

AI in the Research & Design Process

AI is changing enough of the UX process quickly enough that staying current takes real effort. These are the areas I've been paying closest attention to — as a practitioner using AI tools in my own work, and as a designer thinking about what it means to design for AI-powered products.

AI-Assisted Research

There's genuine value in using AI for qualitative synthesis — pulling themes from transcripts, comparing findings across sessions, flagging gaps. The part that still requires a researcher is knowing what the patterns actually mean and whether the framing they suggest holds up against what you observed in the room.

AI-Augmented Design

Generative tools can speed up ideation considerably, which is useful. Where they're less reliable is in decisions that depend on understanding the user's context — AI can produce variations quickly, but evaluating them still requires someone who knows what the work actually involves.

Designing AI Experiences

AI-powered features are an interesting design problem because the system's behavior isn't fully predictable, which changes how you think about trust, feedback, and errors. Users need enough transparency to calibrate their trust appropriately — not so much that the uncertainty becomes noise.
