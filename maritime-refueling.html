Maritime Refueling

Enabling better coordination for ad hoc ship refueling negotiations at sea.

Case study visuals available on request

The Project

Ships at sea frequently need to negotiate unplanned fuel purchases with nearby suppliers — an activity that occurs with regularity but has no dedicated tooling to support it. The team behind this project wanted to build a tool that would cover the full negotiation process from start to finish, giving the parties involved a shared environment for reaching agreement. My role was to lead UX research and design across the full arc of the project: definition, research, concept development, and prototype testing.

Definition

On joining the project, I worked with the team to clarify their objectives and map their existing understanding of the current activity. This included producing a diagram of the customer journey as the team understood it at that point — capturing the steps, the actors involved, and where the team believed the friction was concentrated. I then compiled what we needed to know and what we needed to verify, which led to a set of initial questions to take to actual users.

Research

The target users for this project were highly specialized and relatively few in number — and, as practitioners in a commercial field, understandably wary of what might read as a sales pitch. Access was consequently limited to a small number of users for roughly an hour each. That is not an ideal research situation, but it was the realistic one, and it was considerably better than the alternative of continuing to work entirely from assumptions.

Rather than attempting to get a complete account of the full negotiation journey in that limited time, I focused on gaining a high-level view of the key steps and then digging into the areas that seemed most challenging or painful. The method was semi-structured interviews combined with task analysis. The research made one thing quite clear: building a tool intended to support the entire negotiation process from start to finish would mean competing directly with established tools like Outlook and Excel — a scope that did not fit the team's timeline or available resources.

The Pivot

The same interviews that ruled out the broader scope also pointed toward something more tractable. Scheduling — a specific part of the negotiation process involving the coordination of times and locations across multiple parties and communication channels — was a source of disproportionate hassle. The back-and-forth it required, spread across a number of different channels and involving a number of different stakeholders, was consistently identified as a point of friction.

I proposed that the team focus specifically on supporting that activity rather than attempting to cover the full negotiation process. The research provided a clear rationale for the pivot, and the team accepted it.

Concept Testing

Multi-party remote scheduling is a dynamic, time-sensitive activity that is difficult to convey through static mockups. To communicate the concept effectively, I built a visually simple but highly interactive prototype in Axure — one that demonstrated the essential mechanics of how the scheduling interaction would work. The prototype was shown first to the project team and then to the users who had participated in the earlier interviews.

The response was positive. Although I moved to another project at that point, the team continued in the direction the research and prototype had established. The designs produced during this engagement served as the foundation for the product that was eventually released.

Challenges and Lessons

The most significant constraint throughout this project was user access. With such a small sample of participants, the findings could not be validated as thoroughly as would be ideal. Decisions made on the basis of that research therefore carried a degree of risk — which is a real limitation, but also a characteristic of early-stage innovation work, where waiting for a larger or more representative sample is often not a realistic option.

In this case, the risk proved acceptable. The direction the research pointed toward turned out to be viable, and the product that emerged from it was released. The central lesson is that limited user access does not eliminate the value of research — it simply requires being more deliberate about where to focus the time and attention that access allows.
