Maritime Refueling

Enabling better coordination for ad hoc ship refueling negotiations at sea.

The Project

Ships at sea frequently need to negotiate unplanned fuel purchases with nearby suppliers — a process that involves a number of stakeholders, spans multiple communication channels, and has no dedicated tooling to support it. The team had a broad ambition: to build a tool that would cover the full negotiation process from start to finish, improving coordination and reducing friction at every stage.

My Role

I led UX research and design across the full project, including the initial definition phase, user research, concept development, and prototype testing. The work proceeded from a relatively open brief toward a more focused product direction, largely as a result of what the research revealed.

Definition

On joining the project, I worked with the team to clarify their objectives and map their existing understanding of the activity — including a diagram of the customer journey as the team understood it at the time. I then compiled what we needed to know and what we needed to verify through research, which produced a set of initial questions to take to users.

Research

The target users were highly specialized and relatively few in number, and they were appropriately wary of conversations that might turn into sales pitches. Access was limited to a small number of users for roughly an hour each — not ideal, but necessary given that the team was working primarily on assumptions rather than direct knowledge of how the process worked in practice.

Rather than attempting to get a complete overview of the full negotiation journey in the time available, I focused the interviews on a high-level view of the steps and then dug into the areas that appeared most challenging or painful. The method combined semi-structured interviews with task analysis, which allowed me to capture both the structure of the activity and the friction points within it.

The research made clear that building a tool to cover the entire negotiation process would mean competing directly with established tools — Outlook, Excel, and others — that users already had workflows built around. That was a scope that did not fit the team's timeline or resources, and it would have placed the product in a difficult competitive position from the outset.

The Pivot

The interviews also revealed that scheduling was a specific part of the negotiation process that caused a disproportionate amount of friction — primarily because of the back-and-forth communications it required across multiple channels between a number of different stakeholders. I proposed that the team focus on supporting that particular activity rather than the process as a whole. A narrower scope meant a more achievable product and a clearer value proposition.

Concept Testing

Multi-party remote scheduling is dynamic and difficult to convey through static mockups — the interesting part of the concept is how it behaves over time, not what any single screen looks like. I built a visually simple but highly interactive prototype in Axure to demonstrate the concept in a way that could be understood by both the team and the users interviewed earlier.

The prototype was shown first to the team and then to the original user participants. It was well received. Though I moved to another project at that point, the team pursued the direction, and the designs served as the foundation for the product that was eventually released.

Challenges and Lessons

User access was very limited throughout the project, which meant that decisions grounded in research were necessarily based on a small sample. With so few participants, it was not possible to validate findings as thoroughly as would have been ideal — a small sample can reflect genuine patterns, but it can also reflect the particular experiences and perspectives of the individuals involved.

That level of uncertainty is, however, part of the innovation process. Research with limited access is still considerably more grounded than no research at all, and in this case it produced a direction that the team was able to build on successfully.

The central lesson: limited user access does not eliminate the value of research — it simply requires being more strategic about where to focus the time available, and more transparent about the confidence one can place in the findings.
